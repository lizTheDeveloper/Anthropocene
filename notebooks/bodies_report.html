What American bodies carry

Federal health surveys measure pesticide exposure and nutritional status in the same people. Read that way, two things separate cleanly: what Americans share, and what they don't.

1.9×
Children aged 3–11 carry roughly twice the adult body burden of every pesticide measured — glyphosate, the chlorpyrifos metabolite TCPy, and 2,4-D. The gap holds after correcting for urine dilution. It is not an artifact of children's smaller bodies; correcting for that made it larger.
Finding 01 — Children

The youngest bodies carry the most

Urinary metabolite concentrations put children highest for all three compounds. That is exactly what a well-known artifact would produce: children have less muscle mass, excrete less creatinine, and so show higher concentrations of anything per millilitre of urine — regardless of what they actually took in.

So the finding was tested against its own most likely explanation. Dividing each measurement by urinary creatinine — the standard correction for urine dilution — widened the gap in every case: glyphosate from 1.54× to 1.87×, TCPy from 1.36× to 1.88×, 2,4-D from 1.44× to 1.83×.

The skeptical test strengthened the result. Children are not producing more concentrated urine — they are carrying more.

Body burden across the whole age range

Creatinine-corrected weighted medians, µg/g. The pattern is a J: highest in young children, lowest in working-age adults, rising again after 60.

Why this is a policy fact, not just a number The Food Quality Protection Act of 1996 requires EPA to apply an additional tenfold safety factor for infants and children when setting pesticide tolerances, unless it has reliable data showing a smaller factor is safe. These measurements describe the population that provision exists to protect, and they show that population carrying the highest measured internal dose.
What a urinary metabolite is and is not It is an internal dose marker covering roughly the last day or two — direct evidence that exposure occurred and that the compound entered the body. It is not a measure of harm, and nothing here establishes that any individual was injured. Detection is not toxicity.
Finding 02 — What is shared

Exposure is nearly universal, and income buys no exit

Glyphosate is detectable in 77.6% of Americans tested. The chlorpyrifos metabolite TCPy is detectable in 98.8% — effectively everyone. 2,4-D in 81.8%.

And it is flat across the income distribution. Families below the federal poverty line and families at four times the poverty line differ by under three percentage points for glyphosate, and by less than two for TCPy. For 2,4-D the gradient runs slightly the other way — higher-income households show marginally more.

This is not a story about a contaminated underclass. Whatever pathway is delivering these compounds into people — diet, water, drift, household use — money does not meaningfully interrupt it.

Finding 03 — What is not

Nutrition sorts by income, steeply

Run the same income breakdown on nutritional status and the flat lines become staircases.

Iron deficiency among women of reproductive age: 19.2% below the poverty line against 11.9% at four times it. Vitamin D deficiency among adults: 34.7% against 14.1% — a two-and-a-half-fold gap. Folate insufficiency: 25.1% against 12.9%.

Every gradient runs the same direction, monotonically, across all four income bands. By race and ethnicity the iron gap is as stark: 21.6% of Mexican American women and 21.1% of Black non-Hispanic women are iron deficient, against 11.4% of white non-Hispanic women.

Put beside the previous chart, the shape of the problem is legible. Contamination is shared. Nutrition is rationed.

Ferritin undercounts Ferritin rises with inflammation, so the conventional 15 ng/mL threshold misses iron-deficient people who are also inflamed — a group that skews toward exactly the populations showing the highest rates here. These prevalences are floors, not central estimates.
Finding 04 — A negative result

Iron status is not getting worse over time

A common argument holds that declining nutrient density in food should show up as worsening nutritional status in people. On iron, in this data, it does not.

Iron deficiency among American women aged 12–49 was 14.0% in 2005–06 and 14.6% in 2021–23 — flat across sixteen years, with no trend. Median ferritin actually rose 19%, from 33.0 to 39.2 ng/mL.

This is reported because it is true, not because it helps. Any argument that food has become less nourishing and that people are measurably worse off for it has to reckon with this series first. The distributional finding above is strong; the temporal one is absent.

What follows

Two problems, not one

These findings do not combine into a single mechanism, and forcing them together would be the fastest way to lose the argument. Nothing here shows that pesticide exposure causes nutritional deficiency. The two distributions have different shapes precisely because they have different causes.

What the data supports is narrower and more useful. Pesticide exposure is a universal condition of American life — measurable in three-quarters to nearly all of the population, with children carrying roughly double the adult burden, and with no income gradient to suggest anyone has opted out. Nutritional deficiency is a distributional failure — stable nationally, concentrated sharply among the poor, and unchanged for two decades.

The first is an argument about a regulatory standard that already exists in statute and is not visibly binding. The second is an argument about food access, and it needs no new toxicology to make.

Limits worth stating before anyone else does These are cross-sectional associations from a complex survey; none of them establish causation. Confidence intervals here ignore the design effect. The pesticide panels are random subsamples with their own weights, correctly applied but smaller than the full sample. Income and race are entangled in the United States and are reported separately rather than disentangled. And detection of a metabolite is evidence of exposure, not of injury.

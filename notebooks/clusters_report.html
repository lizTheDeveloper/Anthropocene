Three ways to cut the data

Clustering food by residue, farmland by chemistry, and compounds by trajectory. Two of the three mostly confirm what an agronomist would already tell you. The one about food does something more useful — it tells you where the variation is.

Cut 01 — Food

Residue load varies 40-fold across the produce aisle

Clustering 63 commodities on how often each of 51 pesticides is detected pulls out six groups, and the spread between them is large. Frozen strawberries average 5.24 distinct residues per sample, with something detectable in 94.8% of them. Asparagus averages 0.04, with a detection in 3.8%.

The clusters that form are real chemistry, not artifacts. Apples, pears, tangerines and oranges group on postharvest packing-house fungicides — thiabendazole in 59% of samples, pyrimethanil 44%. Leafy greens group on pyrethroids, the only cluster where those lead. Berries and tomato paste group together because they run the same fungicide-and-pyrethroid program in the field.

The most useful number here is not the maximum. It is the range inside baby food, where a parent is making a real choice between products on the same shelf.

Read this correctly A detection is not a violation and not a hazard. PDP's own headline finding is that over 99% of samples fall below EPA tolerance, and nothing here contradicts that. What the chart shows is where residues are more and less common — which is a statement about variation available to someone making a choice, not a safety verdict on any food. Every item on this chart is legal to sell and almost all of it is within tolerance.
Why the baby-food spread matters Baby food pears show a detection in 87.3% of samples at 1.79 residues each; baby food sweet potato shows one in 11.2% at 0.11 residues. That is a roughly 16-fold difference between two products in the same aisle, aimed at the same child, at similar prices. Elsewhere in this project, children aged 3–11 were found to carry about twice the adult body burden of every pesticide measured. These two facts are consistent with each other, but this data cannot establish that one causes the other — that would need dietary recall linked to the same individuals.
Cut 02 — Land

American farmland sorts into three chemical regions

Clustering 2,232 counties on the mix of compounds applied — not the amount, which would only rediscover which counties are big — returns three groups that map onto cropping systems anyone in agriculture would recognise.

The Corn Belt group is 1,349 counties led by Illinois, Iowa and Indiana, running glyphosate at 38% of mass over the corn triad of atrazine, acetochlor and S-metolachlor. The cotton and Delta South group is 400 counties led by Texas and Mississippi, distinguished by harvest-aid chemistry — sodium chlorate at 5.6× the national rate, tribufos and ethephon — plus propanil for rice. The fumigant and specialty-crop group is 483 counties in North Carolina, Georgia, Florida and Idaho, built on soil fumigants: metam-potassium at 4.5×, 1,3-dichloropropene at 4.2×, chloropicrin at 4.1×.

This is the least surprising of the three cuts. Its value is as a stratifier: it gives a defensible way to group counties for any later analysis without hand-drawing regions.

Counties in compound-mix space

Each point is a county, positioned by the first two principal components of its centered-log-ratio compound composition (2018). A 900-county random sample is drawn for legibility. The two axes carry 42.6% of total variance, so read this as indicative of the grouping, not as the whole structure.

Cut 03 — Chemistry over time

Half the mass sits in the one group still climbing

Clustering 179 compounds by the shape of their 1992–2018 national use curve — each normalised to its own peak, so a compound going 1→100 lands with one going 10→1000 — returns four trajectory families.

Two of them are the visible history of pesticide regulation. The phase-out family (77 compounds, 20.9 M kg left) declines monotonically to 9% of its early-1990s peak; methyl bromide sits here, falling 41.9 to 0.4 M kg under the Montreal Protocol, which is the cleanest confirmation the cluster is real. The legacy plateau family (49 compounds, 166.4 M kg) fell by half and then simply stopped falling in 2007 — atrazine, metam, 2,4-D.

The fourth family is the one that matters: 45 compounds still at or near peak in 2018, carrying 226 M kg — 54% of all screened mass. Glyphosate leads it, with S-metolachlor, acetochlor, glufosinate and paraquat.

Four trajectory families, 1992–2018

Cluster centroids, each compound scaled to its own peak year before averaging, so the vertical axis reads as "share of that family's own maximum". Labelled directly at the right edge.

One caveat that belongs on the chart Several compounds in the rising family — S-metolachlor, acetochlor, glufosinate — had zero US use in 1992 because they were not yet registered. Their peak-normalised curves therefore start at exactly zero and pull the family's early years down. Part of the apparent rise is registration timing rather than intensification of use, and the curve should be read with that in mind.
What the clustering does not establish

These are gradients, not natural kinds

All three cuts have weak separation. Silhouette scores are 0.40 for commodities, 0.24 for counties, and 0.24 for compound trajectories, against a theoretical maximum of 1.0. Anything in the 0.2s means the data is a continuum that has been cut into pieces, not a set of genuinely distinct populations that clustering discovered.

That is worth stating plainly, because cluster labels acquire a false solidity very quickly. "The Corn Belt cluster" is a useful shorthand for a region of a continuous space. It is not evidence that American counties come in three kinds.

Two individual clusters should also carry warnings. The 44-commodity group in the food cut is a residual bucket — everything whose fingerprint was too sparse to separate — and it is what holds the overall silhouette up. And the two-member group pairing almonds with baby-food pears is joined by a single shared compound and is not interpretable as a food category; it is reported rather than quietly dropped, but no story should be built on it.

A held-out case worth knowing Salmon returned zero detections and was excluded from the clustering. That is not evidence salmon is clean: it is screened on a fat-soluble persistent-pollutant panel that shares no informative analyte with produce, so it has no position in this space at all. Reporting it as the lowest-residue food would be a straightforward error.
